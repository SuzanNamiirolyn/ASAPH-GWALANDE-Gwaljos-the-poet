Gwaljos the Poet: Why We Must Never Let Go of Our Culture

A profile of a poet who carries his roots forward, not as a relic, but as a living voice.

Words and photographs by Namiiro Suzan Lyn

Poetry does not always come from quiet rooms. Sometimes it comes from carrying home in your chest, even when home is far away. Asaph Gwalande, known on stage as Gwaljos the Poet and also called "Just the Poet," is a Congolese poet, spoken-word artist, filmmaker and creative arts advocate based in Uganda. But more than anything, he is someone who believes deeply that our culture is our anchor. We do not replace it. We carry it forward. Congolese by birth, Ugandan by choice, African by heart: that is how he introduces himself.

At a glance

Real name: Asaph Gwalande  ·  Poetry name: Gwaljos the Poet ("Just the Poet")

From: Eastern DRC (Congo), arrived in Uganda as a refugee in 2016

Based: Nakivale Refugee Settlement, now staying in Kololo, Kampala

Roles: Poet, film director and scriptwriter, poetry coach, programs manager, founder of Tiba Ink Organization, director of Kesho Foundation

Themes: African identity, cultural pride, roots, hope and belonging

His Message: Culture Is Not Something We Trade Away

What stands out most in Gwaljos's work is not just his story of displacement. It is his insistence on identity. He speaks openly about how easily we can forget who we are, how western ways are often presented as "better," while our own traditions are dismissed as "old-fashioned" or "behind."

"We are taught to admire other people's cultures while forgetting our own. But culture is not a burden to drop. It is the very thing that holds us up."

His poetry does not reject progress, but it refuses replacement. He says we can walk into the modern world fully dressed in who we are. We do not need to become someone else to succeed. We only need to understand the value of what we already carry.

People sometimes misread him as anti-European, and he is quick to correct that. "Not true. I have European friends. We speak English together, our middle ground. My fight is not against people. It is against erasure. We can learn from them, walk with them, grow with them, but never replace who we are." His guiding principle, passed to him by a mentor, is simple: the more local you are, the more international. Poetry revives languages the world barely knows, and that is how Africa speaks.

From Home to Here: Loss, Streets, and a Voice

Gwaljos was born in the eastern part of the Democratic Republic of Congo, one of nine children. It was a region of beauty, but also of conflict. One peaceful evening, rebel violence linked to the M23 group shattered their world. "We were enjoying the moment as a family. Then they came." He fled with only one brother, David, leaving behind parents, siblings, and everything he knew. They arrived in Uganda in 2016 and settled in Nakivale Refugee Settlement.

Then, years later, in 2020 or 2022, David too disappeared without a word. "I reported at the police. They tried. Nothing. All I am left with are the documents they gave me." Alone, Asaph slept on the streets. That is where poetry found him.

"I met a man who was a poet while I was sleeping on the streets. He told me: 'Do poetry.' And that is how I survived. That is how I went to school."

Poetry became his shelter and his path. In the settlement, he participated in a poetry workshop co-organized by Nora Doukkali, a researcher and artist, and established Ugandan poets from Kitara Nation, Mitch Isabirye and Peter Kagayi. The first encounter focused on writing, literary entrepreneurship and the collective project of a book, and also led to a further partnership with Sikiliza Collective and new opportunities for exposure. Peter Kagayi saw something in Asaph, known on stage as Gwaljos the Poet, and began to back him. "He was interested in what I do, and he started supporting me." That week changed everything.

The Bayimba Stage: "They Didn't Leave. They Just Relocated."

At the 2026 Bayimba Festival, no stranger to the stage (he has been performing since 2023), Gwaljos arrived ready to perform. "When I see a stage, I perform as if it is my last day. I want visibility, not just for me, but for those I left behind in the camp." The poem he carried there was his first and still his defining work: "They Didn't Leave. They Just Relocated."

Bayimba was only the latest in a long line of stages. He has been performing since 2023, including at Coffee At Last and at Africana Hotel for the Mastercard experience sharing event in 2024. In high school he won the UNSA Championships for Regionals two times, and he has performed at the National Theatre in the Verse in Vac 26th Edition Poetry Show.

In it, he challenges what freedom truly means. "We think we are independent," he explains, "but it is written on paper only. We are still enslaved in ways we do not see." The poem calls old cultural beliefs back to life, not to shut the world out, but to stop foreign cultures from replacing the ones we already have. "It is good to explore, to learn new things," he says. "But avoid replacing who you are."

"Don't be ashamed of your name. Don't be ashamed of your language. The more local you are, the more international."

The festival shifted his writing in real time. Seated among the crowd, watching couples, he was moved to compose a romantic poem on the spot, expanding his voice beyond cultural advocacy into love and everyday joy. For Gwaljos, poetry is both a living expression of culture and an art form that brings people together, preserves identity and gives everyday experiences a voice. "I used to write only of patriotism and roots. This place showed me people need to feel seen in their daily lives too. Poetry can educate and delight."

More Than Words: A Foundation, Many Hats

Today, Gwaljos wears many hats. He founded his own community-based organization, Tiba Ink Organization, bringing young refugee poets together to write, create shows and publish books: talented young people who otherwise have no exposure. Today he runs Kesho Foundation, which carries that work forward. Peter Kagai, who leads Kitara Nation, was impressed by his work and brought him into the organization, a Bayimba Foundation partner project that trains poetry in high schools across Uganda. Today he serves as its Programs Manager, overseeing operations across two districts, hiring trainers and nurturing the next generation of voices.

He is also a poetry coach in over twenty schools, including Aisha Girls in Nakivale, an entrepreneurship mentor guiding young creators to sustain their art, and a film director and scriptwriter, a craft he already practiced back in Congo before the war. "Poetry and film help each other. When dialogue speaks with poetic power, the whole story rises."

"I tell young people: your culture is not a museum piece. It is a language. It is a song. It is the way your grandmother held your hand. It is alive in you, until you decide to let it die."

What His Poetry Does

Gwaljos does not write poetry just to make people feel something. He writes to make them remember something. His work pushes back against the quiet message that our traditions are less valuable, less modern, less worthy of pride. He writes from what he has lived, the violence, the loss, the streets, the finding of poetry, and from what he sees: the poets in his group, the children in the schools, the people whose stories are never told. "My story is theirs," he says, "and theirs is mine."

When asked what reaction he hopes for, his answer is simple: "I want them to say, I recognize myself. I want them to stand taller in their own skin. Even one person feeling seen, that is success." One audience member captured it this way: always choose your roots, do not forget where you come from.

The road is still hard. Refugee status means people underlook him, judging before they hear what he can do. Language is a daily barrier: communities around him speak Luganda, and he sometimes feels left out, though he is learning and says he will speak up next time. He holds a refugee ID and hopes to keep his Congolese citizenship while building a full life here in Uganda.

"They tell us: change to fit the world. I tell them: change the world by being fully you. There is power in what is yours, never let anyone convince you otherwise."

Wisdom for Young Creators

To young poets listening, in camps, villages and slums, his advice is direct. "Do not compare your beginning to someone else's middle. Your voice is enough. Post your work no matter what phone or camera you have. Perform anywhere. Be seen." Talent is already inside you, he says; nurture your own soul rather than copying someone else's. His own inspiration is the Ugandan poet Kitaka, proof that greatness is right here, not somewhere far away.

Closing

Gwaljos the Poet is not fighting against change. He is fighting against replacement. He believes we can learn from other cultures without bowing to them. We can grow without uprooting ourselves. We can walk forward, but never alone, and never empty-handed.

"English we learn to speak to the world. But your mother tongue? That is who you are. Don't trade it away."
